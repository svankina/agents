# Crisp Writing

Crisp writing transfers ideas with minimum reader effort. Every unit of energy the reader spends parsing your prose is energy they can't spend on your idea. Fancy writing makes the reader work so the writer looks good — the opposite of the job.

## Core principles

**Write like you talk.** Test each sentence: would you say it this way to a friend across a table? If not, rewrite until you would. Spoken language uses simple words and direct constructions; it's the register people absorb fastest. This single test beats most style rules.

**Make claims as strong as possible without being false.** "Caching helps in some cases" is easy to defend and tells the reader nothing. "Caching cut our p99 from 800ms to 90ms" is strong and checkable. When you must hedge, hedge precisely — "I think" and "usually" encode your actual degree of certainty; they're information, not weakness. Cut hedges that only exist to protect you.

**One idea per unit.** One idea per sentence, one point per paragraph, one topic per piece. When a sentence carries two ideas, split it. When a piece carries two topics, cut one or write two pieces.

**Claim, then evidence — immediately.** Every assertion gets a number, an example, or a story in the next breath. Unsupported claims stack up into prose that sounds confident and says nothing.

**Specifics beat abstractions.** "A large company" → "Stripe." "Significantly faster" → "3× faster." "Recently" → "last Tuesday." Concrete details are what readers remember and repeat.

**Earn every word.** The goal isn't fewest words — it's strongest words. A vivid 20-word sentence beats a flat 10-word one. But each word must do work the others can't.

## Mechanical cuts

These patterns are wordiness with names. Hunt them in revision:

| Pattern | Bloated | Crisp |
|---|---|---|
| Wind-ups | "I think it's worth noting that X" | "X" |
| Empty intensifiers | "really very important" | "critical" |
| Redundant pairs | "each and every" | "every" |
| Phrase → word | "due to the fact that" | "because" |
| Nominalizations | "made a decision to" | "decided to" |
| Prepositional pile-ups | "the reason for the failure of the team was" | "the team failed because" |
| Passive voice | "mistakes were made by the parser" | "the parser made mistakes" |
| Negative → affirmative | "not uncommon" | "common" |
| Implied modifiers | "advance planning", "end result" | "planning", "result" |
| Throat-clearing openers | "In today's fast-paced world..." | (delete; start at the point) |

## Process

1. **Draft fast and bad.** Get the ideas down; don't polish sentences that might get cut.
2. **Lead with the point.** The first sentence should be the thing the reader would quote. Background goes after the point, or nowhere.
3. **Cut ruthlessly.** Expect to delete 30–50% of a first draft. If a sentence can go without losing meaning, it must go.
4. **Read it aloud** (or subvocalize deliberately). Anything that catches — a stumble, a reread — marks a sentence to fix. Done means nothing catches.
5. **End when the point lands.** When the natural ending arrives, grab it. Don't summarize what the reader just read.

## Is it worth writing at all?

Useful writing = important × novel × correct × strong. If the topic matters to nobody, or says only what everyone already knows, no amount of crispness saves it. Anything that surprised you while learning the topic will surprise readers too — lead with that.

## Twitter / X

The platform compresses everything above and adds one brutal constraint: the reader is mid-scroll and owes you nothing.

### Hooks (the first line is most of the game)

Draft 5–10 hooks before picking one. Working hook shapes:

- **Counter-narrative**: a specific claim against conventional wisdom. "Most teams don't need Kubernetes. Here's what 90% should run instead."
- **Surprising specific**: a stat or fact that makes readers think "I didn't know that." Surprise is what gets shared.
- **Earned credibility + promise**: "I reviewed 200 failed YC applications. The same mistake killed half of them:"
- **Open loop**: raise a question the reader must scroll to close. Close it — an unfulfilled loop reads as clickbait.
- **Eloquent articulation**: say the thing your audience feels but hasn't put into words.

Never open with "A thread about X 🧵" or "Some thoughts on X" — that's a label, not a hook.

### Threads

- 5–15 tweets. One complete idea per tweet; each should be quotable standalone.
- Line breaks between thoughts. Dense paragraphs die on mobile.
- Every claim gets evidence in the same tweet or the next one.
- End tweets on small open loops to pull the reader forward.
- The body must overdeliver on the hook's promise — overdelivery is what earns bookmarks; underdelivery reads as bait.

### X articles (long-form)

- Hook rules still apply to the title and first paragraph.
- Bold headers every few paragraphs; short paragraphs (1–3 sentences); images or embedded posts to reset attention.
- Write to one reader ("you"), not an audience.

## Editing mode

When handed text to tighten:

1. Find the strongest sentence — it's usually buried in the middle. Move it to the top.
2. Apply the mechanical cuts table line by line.
3. Run the say-it-to-a-friend test on every surviving sentence.
4. Preserve the author's voice: cut and reorder freely, but don't swap their word choices for yours unless theirs is bloated. The goal is their message, sharper — not your rewrite.
5. Show the result, then briefly note the 2–3 biggest changes and why, so the author learns the pattern rather than just receiving output.
